The question isolates disagreement between services for common endpoints and time periods. Its strength is an observable comparison with a fixed unit and a clear temporal contrast. Its weakness is that services can recommend different streets and apply undisclosed models. Neither estimate is an observed journey. The original Assignment 2 Google Maps–2GIS design was amended after access and publication checks. TomTom–2GIS remains the prospective question, while the completed work concerns the ORS instrument. This distinction prevents successful software operation from being presented as an answer about congestion.

The approach is quantitative secondary-data analysis using repeated observations. Researchers select requests but do not manipulate road conditions, allocate traffic treatments or recruit drivers. It is therefore an observational comparison, not an experiment. Repeated endpoints reduce spatial inconsistency and make variation auditable. However, purposive route selection and a short observation window limit generalisation. Repeated requests on one route are dependent; their number cannot be treated as the number of independent roads. ORS offers a practical control for the collection workflow but lacks the live-traffic signal required by the main question.

The strategy is a bounded Astana case study with purposive sampling of public arterial corridors and landmarks. Common endpoints specify the requested journey rather than forcing a common returned path. A practical consequence is that analysis must preserve both all eligible pairs and a length-comparable subset. Similar distance alone cannot prove street-level equivalence. The design therefore keeps geometry where its licence permits, records route definitions separately, and avoids interpreting differences as provider error. The instrument phase can proceed independently without changing the predeclared traffic hypotheses.

Coordinates were fixed before research collection using public OpenStreetMap endpoint records. Reverse directions are distinct cases because access and road direction can differ. The expected-length column is a spherical straight-line lower bound, not a measured driving distance. It supports a geographic plausibility check and must not be substituted for the returned route length. The sample contains named corridors rather than personal origins. Selection does not give every Astana journey a known probability of inclusion, so the intended conclusions remain conditional on these cases.

The pilot plan uses 3 complete cycles with 300 seconds between the end of one cycle and the next. The scheduled plan rotates 4 routes every 15 minutes within a bounded 48-hour window. Each route therefore has a nominal 75-minute revisit. Scheduler delays remain visible through actual request times. No past observation is backfilled. Reproduction uses the versioned configuration and dependency file; Python 3.12.14 processed this snapshot. A future provider pair must use the same endpoints, car mode and closely aligned request starts.

The design adapts repeated route and time sampling from Elnaggar et al. (2025) and fixed OD comparison from Fu et al. (2023). It does not reproduce their cities, datasets or validation claims. Hodson (2022) motivates giving each difference measure a defined role. The collector is self-developed because synchronized requests, explicit failures, route identifiers and permission controls must share one audit trail. Its command-line interface writes CSV and licensed response files; it has no participant frontend or personal-data database. Confidence comes from recorded pilot outputs and deterministic validation, not from calling the software a methodology.

This is an original study informed by earlier methods, rather than a full or partial replication of one published protocol. The team combines its question, route set, collection controls and evidence pipeline. No paper supplies an exact template whose result is being reproduced. Derrow-Pinion et al. (2021) and Soon et al. (2023) provide forecasting context, not evidence that the reviewed providers deploy identical algorithms. The ten-paper Assignment 2 corpus is retained so that design provenance remains traceable without claiming implementation details that those sources do not establish.

The storage layout separates original records from the working copy used for interpretation. Raw CSV rows retain source files and request identifiers; processing adds validation decisions instead of overwriting observations. A version manifest records input hashes and software versions. This makes a later report reproducible even when new scheduled records arrive. The research journal records what was done, by whom, what failed and what changed. The current report is a dated snapshot, while the processed observations and documented procedures form the evidence base for Assignment 4.

For a conceptual road graph G=(V,E) at a fixed traffic snapshot, path cost is T(P,t)=Σ w_e(t), summed over edges e in P; each weight is in seconds. Route selection seeks a permitted low-cost path. This abstraction explains why endpoints alone do not determine an estimate: road access, path choice and time weights may differ. It does not identify TomTom or 2GIS source code. ORS documentation describes a speed model based on mapped road properties; its public service does not supply live traffic. The study evaluates returned quantities without reverse-engineering a proprietary solver.

For an eligible pair, signed difference is A minus B and absolute difference removes its direction. MAE averages absolute differences, whereas RMSE gives larger differences greater influence. Relative difference uses the pair mean as denominator, avoiding a claim that either provider is the reference truth. Quartiles describe spread. Planned uncertainty procedures preserve route clusters rather than treating repeated requests as independent. The pilot receives descriptive summaries only. With no primary pairs, disagreement statistics and peak contrasts remain unestimated, rather than being filled with zeros or inferred from ORS variability.

The dictionary distinguishes absent optional fields from missing measurements. An empty no-traffic duration is expected for ORS and is not replaced by a fabricated baseline. Empty error and exclusion fields indicate a successful unflagged record, not poor data quality. Missing counts use each named dataset as their denominator. A paired dataset with no records has no observed missingness percentage. The inventory includes every raw, audited and paired column generated by the processing script.

Pilot success is assessed against the predeclared reliability expectations; it is not a conclusion that one route service is more accurate or that an observed estimate matches a real trip.

The earlier literature supports this restraint. Bastarianto et al. (2025) and Valero et al. (2025) distinguish calculated from reported journeys; neither kind can simply be treated as error-free truth. Boeing and Zhou (2026) and Verma et al. (2025) demonstrate useful reference comparisons without removing that distinction. Mansurova et al. (2025) provide local bus data, but its mode and stopping behavior do not validate private-car estimates. Future interpretation must therefore separate model agreement, sampling coverage and independent journey validation instead of using one as a substitute for another.
